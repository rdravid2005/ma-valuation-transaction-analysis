## 30-second interview explanation

I built an independent M&A model evaluating a hypothetical Rockwell Automation acquisition of Cognex. I forecast Cognex's operating performance, valued it using DCF, trading comps, and precedents, and then modeled an $80-per-share offer with 30% cash and 70% stock financing. The main finding was that Cognex offers a compelling machine-vision capability, but the price is difficult to justify financially: even with $144.5 million of modeled recurring synergies, the deal remains about 3.7% adjusted EPS dilutive in FY2030. ## 90-second project walkthrough

I wanted to demonstrate the analytical work performed in an M&A process, so I selected a hypothetical acquisition of Cognex by Rockwell Automation. Strategically, Cognex would add machine vision, barcode identification, and image-analysis capabilities to Rockwell's industrial automation portfolio.

I first spread Cognex's historical financials and built a driver-based five-year forecast. I then valued the company using a DCF, public trading comparables, and precedent transactions. After comparing those ranges, I tested an illustrative offer of $80.00 per share, which represents a 33.9% premium and approximately $12.7 billion of transaction enterprise value.

Next, I built sources and uses with a 30% cash and 70% stock mix, modeled purchase accounting and incremental amortization, and calculated reported and adjusted accretion/dilution. I also developed bottom-up cost and revenue synergies by function and ramp year. The base case reaches $144.5 million of recurring pre-tax synergies, but that covers only 58.9% of the approximately $245.2 million needed for FY2030 EPS breakeven, leaving the deal about 3.7% adjusted dilutive.

I sensitized the outcome to offer price, synergy realization, stock consideration, and debt cost. Only the most favorable tested price-and-synergy case becomes marginally accretive. I therefore concluded that the strategic logic is credible, but Rockwell should not support the $80 offer solely on the current financial case. The exercise taught me how valuation, financing, purchase accounting, and synergy underwriting interact in an acquisition model.

## Model-specific technical question bank

1. **Why did you value Cognex before choosing an offer price?**  
   Standalone valuation provides an independent range of intrinsic and market value. It prevents the transaction model from using a strategically attractive narrative to rationalize an unsupported price.

2. **Why use DCF, trading comps, and precedents together?**  
   Each answers a different question. DCF estimates intrinsic value from cash flow, trading comps show how public markets value similar companies, and precedents show prices paid for control. Triangulation exposes method-specific weaknesses.

3. **Why is precedent-transaction value often higher than trading value?**  
   A buyer normally pays a control premium and may share expected synergies with the seller. Precedents can also reflect different market cycles, scarcity value, and transaction-specific competition.

4. **Why use EV / EBITDA instead of P / E for operating comparisons?**  
   EV / EBITDA is capital-structure neutral and compares operating value before interest. P / E is affected by leverage, taxes, non-operating items, and share count.

5. **Why exclude acquisition synergies from Cognex's standalone DCF?**  
   Standalone value should reflect cash flows available without the buyer. Adding synergies there and again in the transaction analysis would double count them.

6. **What happens if WACC falls?**  
   The present value of forecast cash flows and terminal value rises, increasing enterprise value, all else equal.

7. **Why does cash increase equity value but not operating enterprise value?**  
   Enterprise value represents the value of core operations available to all capital providers. Excess cash is a non-operating asset added when bridging from enterprise value to equity value.

8. **Why can a growing target still create dilution?**  
   The buyer may pay a high earnings multiple, issue low-earnings-yield stock, incur interest expense, and record purchase-accounting amortization. Growth alone does not offset an expensive purchase price.

9. **Why use significant stock consideration?**  
   Stock protects liquidity and limits incremental leverage, but it increases the buyer's share count and gives target shareholders continuing ownership.

10. **What is a funding source in sources and uses?**  
    A source supplies cash or value to fund the transaction—buyer cash, new debt, or issued equity. Uses show where that funding goes, such as equity purchase price, refinanced debt, and fees.

11. **What creates goodwill?**  
    Goodwill is the residual after purchase consideration is allocated to acquired net identifiable assets and liabilities, including fair-value write-ups and deferred taxes.

12. **Why is goodwill risky?**  
    It is not amortized under U.S. GAAP but is tested for impairment. A future impairment can signal that expected economics were not realized, although it is non-cash when recorded.

13. **Why do finite-lived asset write-ups reduce EPS?**  
    Customer relationships and technology write-ups are amortized, creating incremental expense after closing. Inventory step-up may also temporarily increase cost of sales.

14. **Why distinguish reported and adjusted EPS?**  
    Reported EPS includes all accounting effects. Adjusted EPS may exclude clearly identified non-cash purchase-accounting amortization and nonrecurring integration charges to show recurring economics. Both should be presented transparently.

15. **Why are cost synergies more defensible than revenue synergies?**  
    Management can directly execute headcount, procurement, facility, and public-company-cost actions. Revenue synergies depend on customer adoption, pricing, channel behavior, competition, and sales execution.

16. **What does 58.9% breakeven synergy coverage mean?**  
    Modeled FY2030 recurring pre-tax synergies of $144.5 million cover only 58.9% of the approximately $245.2 million needed for adjusted EPS breakeven. The remaining shortfall is about $100.7 million.

17. **Why do integration costs matter if excluded from adjusted EPS?**  
    They remain real cash outflows and affect liquidity, leverage, and transaction value. Excluding them from a recurring EPS metric does not eliminate their economic cost.

18. **How could Rockwell reduce dilution?**  
    Negotiate a lower price, improve synergy certainty, alter the debt/equity mix, obtain cheaper financing, use contingent consideration, adjust closing timing, or repurchase shares. Financing optimization cannot make an overvalued target inexpensive.

19. **What did the sensitivity analysis show?**  
    The base $80 offer at 100% synergy realization is about 3.7% adjusted dilutive. Only the best tested case—$76 per share and 150% synergy realization—becomes marginally accretive at roughly 0.1%.

20. **What would you diligence most heavily?**  
    Customer-level cross-sell evidence, product overlap and roadmap, revenue-synergy attribution, engineering retention, channel conflict, integration ownership, working-capital needs, and the valuation of acquired intangible assets.

## Final understanding checklist

Before claiming the project in an interview, be able to do each item without reading the workbook:

- Explain the strategic rationale and the strongest counterargument.
- Bridge equity value to enterprise value.
- Explain how the offer price, premium, and transaction value were calculated.
- Describe the differences among DCF, trading comps, and precedents.
- Walk from revenue to unlevered free cash flow and explain each major assumption.
- Explain WACC, terminal value, and the effect of changing each input.
- Walk through sources and uses and explain why sources equal uses.
- Explain cash, debt, and stock consideration tradeoffs.
- Explain purchase accounting, intangible write-ups, deferred taxes, goodwill, and amortization.
- Walk from buyer standalone net income to pro forma EPS.
- Explain why this transaction is dilutive despite target growth and synergies.
- Distinguish reported EPS from adjusted EPS.
- Defend each synergy category, timing assumption, and realization risk.
- Calculate and interpret breakeven synergies.
- Explain the offer-price, synergy, financing-mix, and interest-rate sensitivities.
- State the base recommendation and identify evidence that would change it.
- Identify the model's most important limitations and hypothetical assumptions.
- Locate every material source and distinguish sourced facts from assumptions.
